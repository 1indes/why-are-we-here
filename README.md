# Essay on Creative Programming: Why Are We Here?

## Introduction

I am going to be very honest --before starting at PolyU, I used to comfortably say the following to anyone who would listen: 
1. I hate AI (and will never use it)
2. I have no interest in programming or even learning how it works
3. I cannot see the use for either these things in an industry outside of computer science, especially when it comes to art, design and other creative fields. 

But now, I am two weeks in, and already I must admit that all of these strong opinions no longer quite hold true. For starters, I have used a lot of AI -- particularly to understand what on earth is being spoken about in this Creative Programming course. And because it has helped me enough that I was able to set this repo up without (major) panicking, I don't think it's fair to say I simply hate it anymore. Secondly, despite my inherent ADHD-like tendency to avoid doing more than the bare minimum in a subject that does not interest me from the get-go, it is safe to say that I am at least a little bit intrigued by programming now, especially after experiencing the thrill of running code and it actually being successful. But none of this is THAT surprising -- I think this is just what the average person who has never touched a programming class experiences when they actually have to complete an assignment for it. 

What has, nonetheless, surprised me is the shift in the third point -- that coding and creativity don't go together. I now know that this is exactly what this course is trying to challenge, and that it is the same issue this assignment asks me to reflect on. So, do they go together? Can they? Should they? Do I want them to, in my own creative practice?

## How my original stance formed, and why it made sense to me at the time

Personal skepticism → Reflect on your initial belief that coding and creativity don’t mix.

Having grown up with a long line of love affairs (some briefer than others) for most "old-time" media, from The Beatles and 80s films and Y2K aesthetics, to  Monet, Van Gogh, and Escher, and of course literature -- by the time I reached university age, I was bound to have skepticism for the terrifying combination of artificiality and complexity I associated with coding, when all these products of art I loved felt so hand-crafted, raw, imperfect, human and unique. To me, they felt accessible and able to speak for themselves, whilst the idea of an artwork generated by a line of code felt confusing, deeply manufactured/automated, even alien. Furthermore, before I had even completed my first year at a liberal arts college in The Netherlands, ChatGPT was already a big part of the conversation whenever anyone talked or worried about plagiarism and academic integrity. And I was surrounded by a group of people outside of computer science, and the general consensus was quite often that it is better not to use it at all. Which one can understand -- in a system where the "essay" was such a consistent form of assessment, it did not seem fair to allow people to immediately circumvent the process of actually writing it themselves. In short -- creativity from a machine became deeply associated with laziness, or even stripped of the label of creativity completely. 

And it's not just my classmates that shared this view -- a Scientific American article from 2024 (a few months after starting my third year of the programme) explains how, when asked how they would feel if they learned their favourite work of art was AI-generated, 62% of a survey group said they would like the artwork less (Scientific American, 2024). Furthermore, the article underlines the reality that even when people aren't completely against the use of AI overall, they still prefer human involvement in creative works.

In short, I didn't feel like I was alone in being skeptical of connecting new technology to the ancient human act of creativity. And I believed that for this ancient act deserved to be preserved now that AI was filling every space, avoiding the grey areas completely seemed safer.

## Why my new stance is more realistic for today's creative industry

However, despite what I felt was an overall opposition to AI and coding taking over creative industries, the industries themselves were already transformating in real time. While I was refusing to use AI for something as simple as an essay outline, AI was actively "reshaping" the creative economy (Paris, 2025) -- but not necessarily by replacing humans. Rather, there is evidence that in many creative industries, AI was "amplifying production" and "intensifying competition". As a matter of fact, the industry opinion is much more complex, because these technologies, while usefully streamlining the creative process at a low cost, also raise questions around intellectual property and artistic integrity. Thus, AI is not a taboo, but there is a strong sense that human creativity should be supported, rather than sidelined, by it. (Paris, 2025)

Ultimately, I would also be a hypocrite if I said I hadn't touched AI in recent times to help me in creative processes -- including the process of setting up this very repo, allowing me to write in this very file. For the technical side of it, I genuinely would not have been able to understand what I needed to do step by step without panicking. For the creative side of it, I knew I needed structure, even if I was going to flesh it out myself -- hence, I used AI to draft a rough outline referring to the sources I had chosen. 

And as for a feeling that coding could not reflect the raw humanity embedded in true creativity, after watching The Art of Code by Dylan Beattie for this class, I would be lying if I said I was not enthralled by not just the layers of creativity in the programming world, but by the passion I saw in the speaker for what he does, the same passion I had observed in the traditional art forms I already loved. 

This really made me begin to question my original stance. Because if it was inevitable that AI-generated art would in one way or another bleed into the creative world, how could the refusal to use these tools, provided it was in a way that did not forget about human creativity, allow artists to be visible amongst the sheer scale of heavily AI-influenced or purely AI-generated art? It was not easy for me to admit to myself, but it was impossible to ignore the idea completely. Artists would have to engage with AI tools not only to keep up in volume with purely AI-generated "slop" -- they would have to do so to ensure that AI is used as a supporting, not replacing, tool. While I did not want the craft involved in the traditional art I loved to die, I realized that it would if they were completely left out of the wave of new technology. What if there was a way to connect the traditional artist to the programmer? Could this be what I would learn in this course? I certainly hope so.
